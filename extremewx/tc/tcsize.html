Tropical Cyclone Potential Size

Potential size is to storm size what potential intensity is to storm intensity: the size a tropical cyclone is trying to reach, set by its environment alone. A storm expands because eyewall latent heating outruns radiative cooling, driving a low-level inflow that imports planetary vorticity and spins up the outer circulation. On the f plane that gives a potential size scaling like 1/f — far larger than real storms, and predicting a steep decrease with latitude that nature does not show. On the sphere, β gyres ventilate the vortex and spin its outer circulation back down, collapsing the potential size to r8 ≈ 500–700 km almost independently of latitude and SST, as observed — which is what makes it computable for the real Earth. Move the sliders and watch the blue (f only) and red (with β) predictions diverge. Nothing is uploaded; everything is computed from the published equations. Email me if you spot a bug.

The two competing terms

Everything in this model comes down to a tug-of-war in the tangential wind budget at the target radius.

Schematic after Fig. 1 of WC26 (Northern Hemisphere). The heating-driven axisymmetric inflow (blue) imports planetary vorticity and spins the outer circulation up. The β-gyre ventilation flow (red) crosses the vortex from southeast to northwest, and its Coriolis acceleration spins the axisymmetric circulation down.

$$\frac{\partial \overline{v}_s}{\partial t}\approx \underbrace{-f_c \overline{u}_t}_{\text{inflow}} \underbrace{-\,f u_g}_{\beta\ \text{gyres}} \underbrace{-\,\frac{C_d(\mu v_t)^2}{h_w}}_{\text{friction}}$$

The radius of a fixed wind speed then moves at

$$\frac{dr_t}{dt}=\frac{\partial \overline{v}_s/\partial t}{-\,\partial \overline{v}_s/\partial r}\bigg|_{r=r_t}$$

Volume-integrated cooling grows like rt2 while latent heating grows more slowly, so the first term decreases with size; the β term grows like β3/2rt2. Their crossing point is the potential size.

What this is

A browser implementation of the theory of tropical cyclone potential size — the outer size a storm is trying to reach, set by its environment alone, in the same spirit that potential intensity sets the intensity a storm is trying to reach. Given environmental parameters only, the model is fully predictive and fully analytical: no numerical simulation is involved anywhere on this page.

Three papers sit behind it. Wang, Lin and Chavas (2022) introduced potential size and solved for it from the energetics of the most efficient thermodynamic cycle. Wang and Chavas (2024) derived it mechanistically from the size-expansion problem — why a storm expands, how fast, and why it stops — but with f only, which yields a potential size scaling like 1/f that is far too large in the tropics. Wang and Chavas (2026) adds the dynamical effect of β, and is therefore the version that can actually be evaluated for the real Earth. Both are computed side by side on this page.

The model, end to end

1. Thermodynamics. The Carnot velocity, the bulk static stability, and the two parameters that set the heating and cooling:

$$V_{Carnot}^2=(\eta\,\epsilon_C L_v - R_v T_{SST})\,q^*_{vs},\qquad \epsilon_C=\frac{T_{SST}-T_{tpp}}{T_{SST}},\qquad \eta=0.4 \tag{WC24 29}$$
$$\Delta s_d=\frac{L_v q^*_{vs}}{T_{SST}},\qquad A=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{\epsilon_{p,ew}L_v q_{vb}}{\alpha_p T_{e,lat}\Delta s_d},\qquad B=\frac{1}{2}\frac{c_p\,\Delta p\, Q_{cool}}{\rho_i\, g\, T_{e,rad}\Delta s_d} \tag{WC24 11}$$

A is a dimensionless latent-heating parameter, B a velocity set by radiative cooling, and Δp the effective mass of the cooled layer between ps and pt.

2. Potential size with f only (WC24), via the equilibrium eyewall updraft mass flux:

$$\sqrt{\left(\frac{M_{ew}}{\rho_w}\right)_{eq}}=0.79\,\sqrt{\pi w_{cool}}\;C_d^{-0.07}\,\frac{V_{Carnot}}{f} \tag{WC24 30}$$
$$r_{RCE,eq}=\sqrt{\frac{A}{B}\left(\frac{M_{ew}}{\rho_w}\right)_{eq}},\quad \xi=\frac{C_d\mu^2}{Bf},\quad r_{t,eq,f}=\frac{-\xi v_t^2+\sqrt{\xi^2v_t^4+4r_{RCE,eq}^2}}{2} \tag{WC24 15--17}$$

3. β gyres and the shrinking term. The β-gyre streamfunction is expanded in wavenumber-1 Fourier–Bessel modes and closed with the empirical β-drift scaling:

$$\theta_g=\theta_d^*-\frac{\pi}{2},\qquad \frac{V_d^*}{R}=4292\,\tilde r_t\,\beta^{0.82},\qquad \theta_d^*=0.74\pi \tag{WC26 8, 29}$$
$$-f u_g=-K_g r_t^2,\qquad K_g=\frac{1}{2}\beta\frac{V_d^*}{R}\cos\theta_g\; \frac{\dfrac{2}{k_{1,1}\tilde r_t}J_1(k_{1,1}\tilde r_t)-J_0(k_{1,1})}{\tilde r_t\,[1-J_0(k_{1,1})]} \tag{WC26 9, 10}$$

where k1,1 = 3.8317 is the first positive root of J1. Since Vd* ∝ β0.82rt, the shrinking term goes roughly as β3/2rt2 — negligible for small storms, dominant for large ones.

4. Expansion rate and potential size on the sphere (WC26). Collecting terms gives one ODE:

$$\frac{dr_t}{dt}=\frac{2s\xi_0 v_t\,r_t}{2r_t+\xi_0 v_t^2} \left[\frac{f_cB}{h_w}\left(r_{t,eq,f}-r_t\right)-K_g r_t^2\right] \tag{WC26 14}$$
$$r_{t,eq,b}=\frac{\sqrt{\left(\frac{f_cB}{h_w}\right)^2+4K_g\frac{f_cB}{h_w}r_{t,eq,f}}-\frac{f_cB}{h_w}}{2K_g}, \qquad K_\tau=\frac{\tau_{r_t,b}}{\tau_{r_t}}=\frac{f_cB/h_w}{K_g\,(r_t-r_{t,eq,u})} \tag{WC26 15b, 22}$$

As Kg → 0 the bracket reduces to the WC24 f-plane form and rt,eq,b → rt,eq,f. Because Kτ < 1 always, β shortens the expansion time scale as well as shrinking the potential size. The solution is symmetric about the equator, so the Southern Hemisphere behaves identically with all signs flipped.

Validation

Implementation notes

  • Panel (a) integrates WC26 Eq. (14) with fourth-order Runge–Kutta rather than evaluating the implicit analytical solution WC26 Eq. (16); the two agree to plotting accuracy.
  • Bessel functions J0, J1 use the Abramowitz & Stegun polynomial approximations, max error 5×10−8 over the range used here.
  • Saturation vapour pressure follows Bolton (1980) evaluated at ps = 1015 hPa, while Δp uses ps = 1000 hPa and pt = 100 hPa, as in WC24.
  • Latitude is used as |φ|; the model is symmetric about the equator, so the Southern Hemisphere is the mirror image.

Caveats worth knowing

  • Vd*/R and θd* are semi-empirical, fitted to barotropic β-plane simulations in a resting environment. The leading coefficient in WC26 Eq. (29) is dimensional, so other non-dimensional parameters must also modulate β drift.
  • Environmental flow (the −f uE term) and the storm's own β drift across latitudes are neglected, as in the paper's baseline.
  • The model predicts the evolution of a single outer wind radius, not the full wind field, and it is not constructed to guarantee physical consistency of the whole profile.
  • The equations are undefined at the equator; this page limits |φ| ≥ 2°.
  • WC26 finds the potential size with β is not very sensitive to Kg: a ±20% change in Kg moves rt,eq,b by 10% or less. You can test that with the β multiplier and θd* sliders.
