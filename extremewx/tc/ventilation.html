Tropical Cyclone Ventilation Explorer

Vertical wind shear drags low-entropy mid-level air into a storm's core — ventilation — and that air acts as anti-fuel, directly cancelling the surface entropy fluxes that power the hurricane heat engine. A three-term power budget (fuel − anti-fuel − friction) collapses the whole problem onto one equation with two parameters, and that equation predicts the ceiling a storm can reach, the threshold it must clear to develop at all, and how fast it spins up or down. Move the sliders, or click anywhere in the phase space, to explore it. Potential intensity is prescribed here rather than computed. Theory from Tang & Emanuel (2010, 2012), Chavas (2017) and Chavas et al. (2025). Email me if you spot a bug or want a feature.

The math
Write the storm's power budget as fuel minus anti-fuel minus friction, and non-dimensionalize wind speed by the potential intensity, ṽ = v/V p. Everything collapses to one equation with two parameters: Δ(ṽ³) = ṽ − λ̃ − ṽ³ The fuel term is the surface entropy flux C k ρ v (s*₀−s), linear in wind speed — the WISHE feedback. Friction is C d ρ v³, cubic, which is why any storm has a ceiling at all. The anti-fuel term is the shear-driven import of low-entropy air, c ρ V s (s* m−s m), and it is independent of the storm's own intensity — that asymmetry is what creates a lower threshold as well as an upper ceiling. Dimensionally the same statement reads Δ(v³) = V p² v − λ̃ V p³ − v³ The dark wedge on the far left is where ventilation exceeds the entire fuel supply, ṽ < λ̃; there the storm is destroyed outright and Δṽ is set to −ṽ.

2  Power budget right now

The three terms for your storm's current state. A positive residual is surplus power available to spin it up; negative means ventilation and friction together have outrun the fuel supply. Watch the fuel bar grow linearly while friction grows cubically as you raise the intensity.

The math
Bars are the nondimensional terms of Δ(ṽ³) = ṽ − λ̃ − ṽ³. Note the two special points that fall out of it. Setting ∂Δ(ṽ³)/∂ṽ = 0 gives the maximum energetic surplus at ṽ = 1/√3 ≈ 0.577, where Δ(ṽ³) = 2/(3√3). Setting ∂(Δṽ)/∂ṽ = 0 instead gives the fastest change in wind speed, at the much weaker ṽ = 1/(3√3) ≈ 0.192, where Δṽ = 2/(3√3) ≈ 0.385. Rapid intensification plausibly lives between the two.

3  Ventilated potential intensity

The storm's true ceiling as a function of ventilation alone. It falls gently at first, then more steeply, and at VImax = 0.145 the two branches collide and the ceiling collapses discontinuously to zero. That hard cutoff is what makes vPI a sharper ingredient for genesis indices than shear and moisture used separately.

The math
Setting Δ(ṽ³) = 0 gives a cubic whose roots are the two branches: ṽ eq³ − ṽ eq + λ̃ = 0 with the compact trigonometric solution ṽ eq = (2/√3) cos[ ⅓ arccos(−(3√3/2) λ̃) − 2πk/3 ] k = 0 is the stable branch (vPI), k = 1 the unstable one (genesis threshold). Real roots require λ̃ ≤ 2/(3√3) ≈ 0.385; at that value both branches meet at ṽ = 1/√3 = 0.5774 and annihilate in a saddle-node bifurcation. An equivalent algebraic form was given by Komacek et al. (2020) and simplified by Garcia et al. (2024).

no timescale The theory gives the sign and relative magnitude of the tendency, not how many hours it takes. Tang & Emanuel (2012) found this pattern matches observed 24-hour intensity changes in the historical record well, and that the same index skilfully predicts genesis.
Where the physics comes from
Shear tilts the vortex, which shifts deep convection downshear and excites mesoscale eddies. Those eddies transport low-entropy air inward along two pathways: direct mid-level intrusion, and downdrafts that flush subsaturated air into the inflow layer as rain evaporates into it. Either way the boundary layer is stabilized and the eyewall loses buoyancy — a direct subtraction from the surface entropy flux, hence "anti-fuel." The mid-troposphere is where it hurts most: in the tropics, horizontal temperature gradients are weak (small Coriolis, gravity-wave smoothing), so entropy contrasts are almost entirely latent heat, and mid-level air is strongly subsaturated relative to a saturated eyewall. Both shear and mid-level moisture vary sharply over short distances and short times, which is much of why intensity forecasting is hard.
