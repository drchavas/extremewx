Tropical Cyclone Wind Field Explorer

A tropical cyclone's near-surface wind field is captured remarkably well by just a few numbers: the peak wind Vmax, the radius where it occurs Rmax, and an outer size R34kt — the radius of tropical-storm-force wind. Move the sliders and watch how the footprint, the radial profile, the pressure, and the minimum central pressure Pmin all respond. Every curve here is generated live by tcwindprofile, ported to JavaScript. To move a storm like this along a track and see the swath it leaves behind, use the idealized track explorer. The math
The rotating wind is axisymmetric: speed depends only on radius. The footprint is that profile swept around a circle. With asymmetry on, the total wind vector is the rotating wind plus a fraction of the translation vector, rotated ~20° counter-clockwise for surface inflow (Lin & Chavas 2012): V⃗total = V(r) êθ  +  0.55 V⃗trans That 0.55 is the same factor the model uses in reverse to strip motion out of the reported point-max wind and recover the azimuthal-mean Vmax it actually needs.

2  Radial wind profile V(r)

The model builds the profile from four physically distinct pieces that join smoothly. Only Vmax, Rmax, R34kt and latitude go in — everything else, including where the wind finally vanishes, comes out.

The math
The natural coordinate is absolute angular momentum, M = rV + ½fr². Between Rmax and R34kt, M is close to linear in r (Tao et al. 2023) — that single fact fixes the inner core. Outside, the wind falls off like a modified Rankine vortex, then transitions to the Emanuel (2004) balance between surface Ekman friction and radiative subsidence, which forces V to zero at a finite radius R0: r < Rmax:   V = Vmax r/Rmax Rmax–Raa:   V = [(S(r/Rmax−1)+1)Mmax − ½fr²] / r Raa–Rba:   V = A/r Rba–R0:   V = (A−½fRba²)/r + fRba − ½fr The transition radii Raa, Rba and R0 are not tuned — they follow from requiring the branches to match.

3  Pressure profile P(r)

Wind and pressure are two views of the same thing. Integrating gradient wind balance inward from the storm edge gives the pressure dip; its depth at the centre is Pmin. Notice that widening the storm deepens Pmin even at fixed Vmax.

The math
Gradient wind balance relates the pressure gradient to the wind: dP/dr = ρ ( fV + V²/r ) Integrating inward from R0 gives the shape of the pressure deficit. Its absolute depth is set separately by the Chavas, Knaff & Klotzbach (2025) fit, which is why both intensity and size appear: ΔP = −6.60 − 0.0127 Vmax² − 5.506 (½fR34kt) + 109.013 (½fR34kt)/Vmax This is why Pmin alone is a poor measure of intensity: a big weak storm and a small strong storm can share the same central pressure.

4  The Vmax–R34kt plane

Intensity and outer size vary almost independently in nature, so the honest way to see the relationships is over the whole plane. Click anywhere to move the storm there.

The math
Rmax is predicted from outer size using Chavas & Knaff (2022): the ratio Mmax/M34kt of angular momentum at the two radii collapses onto a tight function of Vmax and ½fR34kt, and inverting M = rV + ½fr² then gives Rmax directly: Mmax/M34kt = 0.699 exp[−0.00618 ΔV − 0.00210 ΔV (½fR34kt)] Reading the map: Rmax shrinks steeply as a storm intensifies, and grows with outer size — but the two controls are far from interchangeable. Latitude matters too, through f.

How the model works, and where it comes from

Four steps, each one an observationally validated published result. Nothing on this page is tuned by hand.

  1. Rmax from outer size. Chavas D. R. and J. A. Knaff (2022), A simple model for predicting the tropical cyclone radius of maximum wind from outer size, Wea. Forecasting 37(5), 563–579. doi:10.1175/WAF-D-21-0103.1
  2. The complete radial wind profile. Tao D., Nystrom R., Chavas D. R. and A. Avenas (2025), A fast analytical model for the complete radial structure of tropical cyclone low-level wind field, Geophys. Res. Lett. preprint. This is a fast analytic approximation to the full physics-based model of Chavas, Lin & Emanuel (2015), doi:10.1175/JAS-D-15-0014.1, with outer structure from Emanuel (2004) and Chavas & Lin (2016).
  3. Pmin from intensity and size. Chavas D. R., Knaff J. A. and P. Klotzbach (2025), A simple model for predicting tropical cyclone minimum central pressure from intensity and size, Wea. Forecasting 40(2), 333–346. doi:10.1175/WAF-D-24-0031.1
  4. The pressure profile follows from gradient wind balance applied to the wind profile, rescaled to match that Pmin.

Why start from R34kt rather than Rmax? Because it is the most robustly known size measure we have: routinely estimated operationally, low enough in wind speed for satellites to measure reliably over the ocean, and usually outside the noisy convective inner core.

caveat This is an idealized footprint: a smooth, circular, mature storm over open ocean. Real storms have asymmetric convection, spiral bands, boundary layer rolls, land interaction and shear-induced tilt. The point of a model like this is not to replace an analysis of a specific storm, but to show how much of the wind field is set by just a handful of numbers.
