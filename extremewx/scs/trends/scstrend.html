Guidance on interpreting these data: Storm Events records reports, not observations from a fixed network. National counts rose roughly thirtyfold between 1955 and the late 1990s as population, spotter networks, radar and NWS verification practice changed, so trends spanning that era partly measure the growth of reporting. Trends from about 2000 on are far more defensible, and the higher thresholds are much less sensitive to reporting practice than “any report”. ASOS present-weather and peak-wind reporting likewise varies by site and era, so compare stations with care.

Methods
  1. Sources. Hail, tornado and thunderstorm wind come from the NOAA/NCEI Storm Events Database, using county-type records (CZ_TYPE = C). Freezing rain and peak wind come from NOAA/NCEI Integrated Surface Database hourly observations at ASOS stations.
  2. Locate each report. Storm Events reports are assigned to a county by STATE_FIPS + CZ_FIPS rather than by county name, which avoids name collisions and independent cities. Station hazards stay at their station.
  3. Apply the threshold. Each report is scored against the selected size or intensity cut. Thresholds are cumulative — a 2″ hailstone also counts as a 1″ one.
  4. Collapse to hazard days. Count distinct calendar dates on which a county — or a station — saw at least one qualifying report. One storm producing forty reports in a county on one afternoon is one hazard day.
  5. Screen the station records. A station-year below 90% complete is treated as no observation and dropped, not as zero days. That is the opposite of the county rule below, and both are right: every county exists every year, a station does not. QC stations further restricts to the set of DelPizzo et al. (2025), who kept only sites with an overnight manual observer. Freezing rain opens on that QC'd subset, because automated present-weather sensors report FZRA too erratically to trend; the button adds the rest back if you want to see the difference. Peak wind is measured differently and is not screened this way, so it opens on all stations.
  6. Store those counts per county (or station) × year × month × threshold. Everything below is computed live in the browser from that table.
  7. Climatology map. Mean annual hazard days over the selected period. For counties a year with no days is a real zero and is counted in the denominator; for stations, unobserved years are excluded rather than counted as zero.
  8. Trend map. Ordinary least squares on the annual counts, reported per decade. Significance is a two-sided t test on the slope; stations reaching p < 0.05 are ringed.
  9. Annual and monthly panels. For a selected county, that county alone. For a state, calendar dates on which anywhere in the state qualified — deliberately not the sum of county-days, since one storm can cross twenty counties in an afternoon. For stations with none selected, the mean over those observed in every year of the period, so a station entering or leaving the record cannot masquerade as a trend.
  10. Colour scales. Set from the data on screen: robust upper percentiles for climatology, symmetric about zero for trend.
  11. Derecho events work differently — they are single storms, not a climatology, so that mode shows one map and no trend. The dates come from Shourd and Kaplan (2025); everything else is computed here from Storm Events. Their paper catalogues 147 warm-season derechos but prints dates for only its 25 derecho series and two case studies, so 54 are listed. Reports on the date are single-linked in space and time (within 150 km and 3 h) and the largest resulting group — the one carrying the most ≥64 kt gusts — is taken as the swath; the envelope is its convex hull at the selected threshold, and the times are the first and last report in it, converted to UTC. Report counts run above the paper's, which used SPC filtered reports that thin near-duplicate reports of one gust.
