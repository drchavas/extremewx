Historical Severe Weather Events

Individual severe-weather days, rather than the climatology. Two things live here: the SPC derecho archive of Squitieri, Wade and Jirak (2026), and the biggest days on record for hail, tornadoes and thunderstorm wind — the top 50 for any state and the top 100 nationally, ranked by how many counties reported the hazard — or type any date as YYYYMMDD to map that day directly. Guidance on interpreting these data: Storm Events records reports, not observations from a fixed network. National counts rose roughly thirtyfold between 1955 and the late 1990s as population, spotter networks, radar and NWS verification practice changed, so trends spanning that era partly measure the growth of reporting. Trends from about 2000 on are far more defensible, and the higher thresholds are much less sensitive to reporting practice than “any report”. ASOS present-weather and peak-wind reporting likewise varies by site and era, so compare stations with care. June and July 1993 are missing from the Storm Events database entirely — the NWS offices' records for those two months were lost when NCEI migrated the archive — so 1993 is dropped from maps and annual plots rather than counted as a quiet year.

Methods
  1. Source. NOAA/NCEI Storm Events Database, county-type records (CZ_TYPE = C), plus the SPC derecho archive of Squitieri, Wade and Jirak (2026) for the derecho swaths.
  2. What ranks a day. The number of distinct counties in the chosen place that reported the hazard at or above the selected threshold on that calendar date. Counties rather than report counts on purpose: national report totals rose roughly thirtyfold between 1955 and the late 1990s as population, spotter networks and radar changed, so ranking by reports would largely rank by era and by population. A county either saw it or it did not, which travels far better across seventy years.
  3. How many. The top 50 days for each state and the top 100 nationally, computed separately for every threshold — the biggest ≥ 2″ hail days are a very different list from the biggest “any hail” days. States with fewer qualifying days show all they have.
  4. The day view. Counties shades each county by how many qualifying reports it logged. Reports draws every individual report as a point, coloured by magnitude; that detail is a separate download and is fetched the first time you ask for it.
  5. Any date. The ranked lists are a way in, not a limit: the Date box takes any YYYYMMDD and maps that day, whether or not it ever made a top 50. Every date in the record is carried — 13,879 for hail, 12,741 for tornadoes, 15,446 for thunderstorm wind — against roughly 3,000 ranked ones. A date inside the record with nothing to report says so rather than drawing a blank map, and so does one outside it.
  6. Ties are broken by date, earliest first.
  7. Reporting practice dominates long comparisons. A 1960s day and a 2020s day are not measured the same way. Within the last two decades or so the lists are much more comparable. The higher thresholds are far less sensitive to this than “any report”.
  8. June and July 1993 are missing from Storm Events entirely — NCEI lost those records — so no day in those two months can appear in any list.
